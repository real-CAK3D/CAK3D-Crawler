Spiders

Spiders are air-breathing arthropods with eight legs, chelicerae that end in fangs, and spinnerets that pull out silk. They form the largest order of arachnids, with more than 50,000 described species, and they live on every continent except Antarctica.

Anatomy

A spider's body has two main parts. The front part, the cephalothorax, carries the eyes, mouthparts and all eight legs. The rear part, the abdomen, holds the silk glands and is joined to the front by a narrow waist called the pedicel.

Most species have eight simple eyes, though many see poorly. Jumping spiders are the exception: their large front eyes give them sharp vision for judging a leap. Spiders breathe through book lungs, tracheae, or both. Their legs have flexor muscles but no extensors; they straighten their legs by pumping hemolymph into them, which is why a dead spider's legs curl inward.

Silk

Silk starts as a liquid protein called spidroin stored in the silk glands. As it is drawn through the spinnerets it hardens into a fibre. A single orb weaver can make up to seven kinds of silk, each for a different job: safety lines, egg sacs, wrapping prey, or the sticky capture spiral.

The strongest kind, dragline silk, is stronger than steel of the same weight and tougher than Kevlar. Many spiders eat their old web before spinning a new one, recycling the protein. Young spiders also use silk to fly: they let out a thread, catch the wind, and drift for kilometres in a behaviour called ballooning.

Webs

Web shapes vary by family. Orb weavers build the classic wheel. Sheet weavers lay down flat mats, cobweb spiders spin tangled 3D nets, and funnel-web spiders wait at the end of a silk tube.

An orb web goes up in a fixed order. The spider first floats a bridge line across a gap, then adds the frame, then the radial spokes, then a temporary spiral that works like scaffolding. Last comes the sticky capture spiral, laid from the outside in. The largest orb webs belong to Darwin's bark spider of Madagascar, whose bridge lines can span rivers 25 metres wide.

Hunting without webs

About half of all spiders never build a capture web. Wolf spiders run down prey on the ground. Trapdoor spiders ambush from a burrow with a hinged lid. Spitting spiders pin insects with a spray of venom-laced glue, and Portia jumping spiders plan detours to sneak up on other spiders.

Nearly all spiders use venom, but only a few are dangerous to people. These include the widow spiders, recluse spiders, and the Sydney funnel-web.

Spiders and people

Fear of spiders, arachnophobia, is one of the most common phobias. Spiders also run through myth and story: the Greek weaver Arachne, the West African trickster Anansi, and the barn spider of Charlotte's Web. Researchers study silk as a model for new biomaterials.

The spider also named a piece of the internet. Search engines send out programs called web crawlers, such as Googlebot, that read a page, collect its hyperlinks, and follow them to the next page. Following link after link, they map the World Wide Web the way a spider covers a gap with thread.
